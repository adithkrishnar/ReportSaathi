## 3\. What AI chatbots already do

Published research, regulatory guidelines, and API terms of service delineate clear capabilities, operational failures, and privacy risks associated with general-purpose Large Language Models (LLMs) when handling patient laboratory data:

* **Text Parsing and Multimodal Processing Capabilities**: LLMs demonstrate strong technical performance in extracting text from document photos/PDFs, translating medical terminology, and organizing raw lab parameters into structured summaries.
* **Explicit Prohibition Against Clinical Advice and Practice**: Gemini API Additional Terms explicitly mandate that developers may not use API services in clinical practice, to provide medical advice, or in any manner overseen by or requiring clearance from a medical device regulatory agency. Terms clarify that generated content does not constitute medical treatment or diagnosis.
* **Hallucination Risks and Unbounded Reference Ranges**: General AI chatbots lack strict functional guardrails, creating risks of hallucinating medical diagnoses, recommending dosage changes, or comparing patient values against generic external reference ranges rather than the specific lab's printed range.
* **Privacy Violations and Human Review on Free Tiers**: Gemini API Additional Terms establish that on unpaid tiers (e.g., Google AI Studio and free API quotas), submitted prompts, uploaded files, and generated outputs are reviewed by human annotators and used to train Google products. Users are explicitly warned not to submit sensitive personal health information.
* **Data Privacy Protection via Paid Enterprise Infrastructures**: Paid API tiers operate under Data Processing Addendums where prompts and responses are not used to train machine learning models and are logged transiently only for policy enforcement.
* **Absence of Actionable Patient Activation**: General AI models generate lengthy, unformatted text blocks rather than structured, actionable checklists that prepare patients to ask meaningful questions during clinical visits.
* **ReportSaathi's Strategic Advantage**: ReportSaathi leverages paid, privacy-compliant AI infrastructure to deliver a strictly bounded, non-clinical explanation layer. It eliminates hallucination risks by enforcing zero-diagnosis guardrails, mapping values strictly to the lab's printed reference range, translating explanations into Indian regional languages, and generating a customized 3–5 question checklist for doctor visits.

## 4\. Complaint patterns

Synthesis of the notebook sources reveals four primary complaint patterns expressed by patients and caregivers regarding medical documents and clinical communication, ranked by the number of supporting sources:

1. **Difficulty Comprehending Lab Numbers, Medical Jargon, and Prescription Instructions** (6 sources/notes)

  * *Handwritten Prescriptions Study (Das et al., 2025)*: Frequency abbreviations like BD, TDS, or HS "were often left unexplained and misunderstood" (8 words).
  * *Mumbai OPD Study (John &amp; Bhate, 2019)*: 52% of patients had "no knowledge to little knowledge" (5 words) of prescription dose, timing, and side effects.
  * *Mangalore REALM Study (Kamath et al., JCDR)*: Patients struggle with high-school reading level materials and "conceal their inabilities out of embarrassment" (6 words).
  * *US EHR Portals Study (Zikmund-Fisher et al., 2014)*: 62% of low-literacy patients cannot identify out-of-range results, leaving numbers "literally meaningless" (2 words).
  * *Lab Literacy Study (Franco &amp; Morris, 2023)*: Patients self-report "difficulty understanding the laboratory data" (4 words) in online portals.
  * *ReportSaathi Problem Definition Note*: Medical documents are filled with "clinical jargon, abbreviations, and numbers that are hard to interpret" (11 words).
  * *Survey response count*: Unverified (not in sources).
2. **Language Barriers and Absence of Vernacular Medical Information** (3 sources/notes)

  * *Handwritten Prescriptions Study (Das et al., 2025)*: Patients recommended "use of vernacular language prescriptions and pictorial aids" (7 words).
  * *Mangalore REALM Study (Kamath et al., JCDR)*: Patient education materials written in complex English exceed patient reading comprehension.
  * *ReportSaathi Problem Definition Note*: Expresses need for translating medical documents into "the user's preferred local language" (6 words).
  * *Survey response count*: Unverified (not in sources).
3. **Rushed Doctor Consultations and Limited Explanation Time** (3 sources/notes)

  * *Mumbai OPD Study (John &amp; Bhate, 2019)*: Patients receive inadequate instruction due to "heavy patient load in the outpatient department" (8 words).
  * *Lab Literacy Study (Franco &amp; Morris, 2023)*: Primary care practice shows a "national decline in the number of primary care visits dedicated to reviewing laboratory results" (14 words).
  * *ReportSaathi Problem Definition Note*: Doctors have insufficient time during crowded OPD consultations to explain raw lab numbers.
  * *Survey response count*: Unverified (not in sources).
4. **Anxiety and Panic Triggered by Online Searches &amp; Misinterpretation** (2 sources/notes)

  * *Lab Literacy Study (Franco &amp; Morris, 2023)*: Misinterpreting out-of-range lab results causes "unnecessary anxiety" (2 words) for patients.
  * *ReportSaathi Problem Definition Note*: Patients viewing raw numbers experience "panic over minor, harmless variations" (5 words) after searching online.
  * *Survey response count*: Unverified (not in sources).

### List of What is Still Unproven

1. **Document Readability (OCR Accuracy)**: Whether smartphone OCR can reliably extract text from crumpled, poorly lit, or messy handwritten Indian prescriptions without misinterpreting decimal points or drug names.
2. **Strict Grounding in Printed Reference Ranges**: Whether LLM prompt architecture can consistently restrict comparisons to wildly varying printed lab reference ranges across thousands of independent local diagnostic labs without leaking external reference ranges.
3. **User Discipline Around Safety Boundaries**: Whether patients will accept a tool that strictly refuses to diagnose conditions or adjust dosages without abandoning the product.
4. **Patient Activation Without Over-Reliance**: Whether plain-language explanations empower patients to attend doctor visits better prepared rather than treating the app's output as a final clearance to skip follow-up consultations.
